## Hue is an angle

<div class="scene wide" data-scene="pal" data-viewbox="0 0 1600 780"></div>

Notes:
Phase is just an angle. Think of the burst as a dot going round a circle: once round, one
cycle. Carry on that rhythm through the whole line. The colour wiggle goes at the same speed but
it's ahead, here by a third of a cycle, 121 degrees. That angle is the hue, and the size of the
wiggle, the length of the arrow, is the saturation. The Americans got there first, with
NTSC. The scope under the screen shows each arrow's wiggle against the burst. Now add a phase
error between the studio and your sofa, say interference from a reflection of the signal off a
building: watch the orange wiggle slide along. It rotates the arrow, every line the same way, and
your newsreader goes green. Hence the hue knob, and "Never The Same Colour".
PAL, Walter Bruch at Telefunken. Odd lines go out exactly as NTSC: same error, still wrong.
But even lines go out with V upside down, mirrored. The error doesn't care what you sent, so it
nudges that line the same way. Then the set flips it back, and now it's wrong the other way: one
line too yellow, the next too pink. Average each pair with a delay line: averaging two arrows
lands halfway between their tips, which is back on the right hue, just a bit shorter. The errors
cancel.
You lose a little saturation instead of getting the wrong colour.

## Berlin, 25 August 1967

- Funkausstellung, right here in Berlin
- Willy Brandt presses a big red button: colour TV for West Germany
- The button was a dummy
- ...and the colour came on a few seconds _before_ he pressed it

Notes:
And it launched here. The 25th Funkausstellung in West Berlin, 25 August 1967. Willy Brandt,
then foreign minister, presses a big red button to start colour broadcasting. The button wasn't
connected to anything, and the broadcast went to colour a few seconds early, before he'd even
pressed it. Which, for a talk at a programming conference, feels about right.
